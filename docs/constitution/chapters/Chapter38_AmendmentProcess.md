## Executive Summary

Chapter 38 opens the closing block by stating the **amendment process** — how the
Constitution itself is changed. Consistent with the whole document's design, the
Constitution grows chiefly by extension and is amended only rarely, deliberately,
and on the record. This chapter states what requires an amendment, who may make
one, how it is recorded, and the one thing an amendment can never do.

## 1. Amendment Statement

An amendment is a **deliberate change to an approved part of the Constitution**. The
amendment process makes such change orderly, authorized, and recorded, so the
Constitution can adapt without losing the stability that gives it force.

## 2. Extension versus Amendment

The Constitution distinguishes **extension** from **amendment** (Chapter 10). Adding
a new chapter, a new work package, or new material to an open ledger is extension,
and it is routine. Changing the meaning of an already-approved chapter or ruling is
amendment, and it is exceptional. Most growth is extension; amendment is reserved
for genuine change of settled meaning.

## 3. What Requires an Amendment

An amendment is required to alter an approved chapter's stated principle, to change a
recorded Director ruling, or to move a boundary the Constitution defines. Routine
additions, clarifications that do not change meaning, and new chapters do not require
an amendment — they are extensions. When in doubt, a change that alters settled
meaning is treated as an amendment.

## 4. Authority to Amend

Amendment is a **Director-level act** (Chapter 11). No amendment takes effect without
the Director's explicit authorization, and no role may amend the Constitution
unilaterally by executing work. The authority to change settled meaning is bounded
by the same Constitution it changes (Section 7).

## 5. Amendment Procedure

An amendment follows the same governed discipline as any consequential change: it is
proposed with its rationale, reviewed against the standards and the rest of the
Constitution for consistency, authorized by the Director, and only then adopted. An
amendment that would contradict an unamended chapter is reconciled before it is
adopted, so the document remains coherent.

## 6. Recording Amendments

Every amendment is recorded — in the amendments register and the decision log — with
its rationale and authorization. The record is what keeps the Constitution's history
traceable: a reader can see not only the current text but what changed, when, and
why (Chapter 15).

## 7. Amendments and Safety

No amendment may weaken Trading Safety. The guarantees that the Risk Manager is never
bypassed and that the advisory intelligence never executes are not amendable away;
they are permanent terms of the Constitution (Chapters 07, 36). An amendment that
would breach them is out of bounds regardless of authorization.

## 8. The Stability Principle

The amendment process exists to keep the Constitution **stable**. Because settled
meaning changes only by a recorded, authorized amendment — and rarely — those who
build on the Constitution can rely on it. Amend rarely, extend routinely: this is the
principle that lets the document endure while the system grows.
